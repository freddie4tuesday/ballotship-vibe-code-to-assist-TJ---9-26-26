Set up the exercise

FACILITATOR

New to Ballotship?

The board works like the classic guessing game Battleship. Each team hides seven election sites on a 6×6 grid: polling places, vote centers, a sorting facility, the operations center. The other team can't see where they are. Each round the attacking team aims at a square, and the inject's footprint (the squares it covers) lands there. Any of your sites under it are hit, and a site with every square hit goes offline for the rest of the game. You learn hit or miss for each square, so over the rounds you can work out where the other side's sites are.

An inject is a scenario card: one realistic problem dropped into the exercise, like a flooded polling place or a fake text telling voters the election moved.

The board is only half of it. The other half is what your team writes in response, and that's what you review together afterward.

Two jurisdictions, seven sites each. Every round one team plays the adversary, draws an inject and picks a target. The other team writes how it responds. Then the inject lands, and any site under it goes offline. Whoever has knocked out more of the other jurisdiction when the rounds run out wins.

There are no dice. A hit is a hit. The quality of a response is judged in the debrief, not by the game.

The injects run in a fixed order, the one set in the scenario workbook. Every inject carries an AI assignment: a team has to build part of its attack with an AI tool before it can commit.

In simultaneous play there is one inject a round and both teams work it at once, first as the adversary and then as the office. Both boards face the same footprint, so neither side gets an easier deck than the other.

If there is a moderator, the moderator sets up the exercise. Otherwise Team 1 does. Everyone else joins with a code.
Each screen runs its own clock, with its own start, pause and adjust buttons. Phase changes start the clocks together.     How it runs

    RULES

    The round, simultaneous

    One inject a round, the same one on both screens. Both teams write an attack from it on the first clock. The attacks swap, and both teams write a response on the second clock. Then the round resolves on both boards at once.

    A screen cannot move to the next phase until the other team's packet has landed. A slow team holds the round rather than pushing the two screens out of step.

    Nobody is cut off when a clock hits zero. Whatever is on the page is committed and the round moves. If no target was picked, the shot goes somewhere at random and the log says so.

    The round, taking turns

    A round has two halves. Team 1 attacks in the first, Team 2 in the second. Each team defends once per round.

    On offense

    Draw an inject. It carries an operational concern and a communications concern. Write how your team runs it, then pick a target cell. The inject's footprint fills from there and anything running off the grid is wasted.

    You learn hit or miss for every cell you fired at, so the board rewards deduction. A hit next to a miss tells you which way the site runs.

    On defense

    You see the inject. You do not see the target. Write your response before the inject resolves. Then it lands, and any of your sites under the footprint go offline for the rest of the exercise.

    Your response does not change what the inject does. It is the record the debrief works from, so write it as though you were briefing your county administrator.

    Crisis injects

    Four injects hit the whole jurisdiction. There is no target and no footprint. Two of your cells go offline and you choose which two, which is the only real decision the defending team gets. Play does not resume until you write a continuity statement.

    The relay code

    In two-screen mode the file writes the code for you and spells it phonetically. An attack code looks like R3-12-D3. A result code looks like R3-H101, meaning the first and third cells you fired at were hits and the middle one was a miss.

    The relay panel

    Bottom right, open on every screen. Everything either team writes or attaches lands there and stays, so the two teams are in contact the whole way through rather than only at the handoff. With an automatic relay switched on, text, links and small files cross by themselves.

    Large files will not fit. Upload one somewhere the other team can reach and paste the link instead.

    The clock

    Every screen runs its own clock, with its own start, pause, add a minute, take a minute and reset, so nothing has to be sent to keep it running.

    They start together because the phases do. When both attacks have been swapped, every screen enters the response phase at the same moment and starts its own response clock.

    When anyone pauses, resumes, adds or takes a minute, or resets, the change is sent to the other screens, and they follow at their next check on the relay, within about 15 seconds. It carries the time left, not just the button that was pressed, so a screen that hears late ends up exactly right and gets back any time that ran on. A clock that reaches zero checks once more before it commits, in case a pause is on its way.

    With one laptop, or two screens taking turns, the clock is a setup option and is off unless the facilitator turns it on. Each attack and each response gets its own time. It waits while the laptop is handed over and starts when the team presses "We have the laptop". At zero, what is written is committed and play moves on.

    The AI piece

    The last box in every attack is the thing you made with AI. Each inject names a specific task and offers a starter prompt if you want one, and the attack will not commit without it.

    Push past another paragraph of text. An image, a screenshot, a voice memo, a mailer, a letterhead, a spoofed alert, a fake headline. The exercise is worth far more when the other team has to look at a convincing fake object and decide what to do about it, and that is the honest version of what an office is up against. Attach it in the relay panel so they can actually see it.

    Do not generate video. Even a short clip takes longer to render than the round allows, and a team waiting on it loses the part of the round that matters. If the inject calls for something on camera, write the script and make the thumbnail instead.

    Start the AI the moment the inject appears. Put one person on the tool while the rest of the table reads the card and argues about the other four boxes. Teams that run it in sequence finish late.

    Fictional county, fictional names, no real people or agencies. Label whatever you make as exercise material. None of it gets posted, and it all goes after the debrief.

    Winning

    When the rounds run out, the jurisdiction with fewer sites offline wins. Tie goes to the one with fewer individual cells down, then to a sudden death round.

    Every site counts the same. A one-cell polling place is as expensive to lose as the four-cell operations center, which means the operations center is the hardest site to knock out and the polling places are the cheapest.

    Breaking, and coming back

    The exercise saves itself as you go. Close the tab, close the laptop, break for lunch, and reopening this page offers to resume where you stopped. Boards, damage, the log and the relay thread all come back.

    Break between rounds, not in the middle of one. Finish the result screen first, so neither side is waiting on a packet from the other.

    It comes back on the same browser on the same machine. A different laptop, a different browser or a private window has no memory of it. Do not clear browsing data over the break.

    Attached files are the one thing not kept. The name and any link stay in the thread, but the file itself is gone, so paste links rather than attaching if a break is coming.

    Export the after-action log at the end anyway. That is the copy you keep.

                How this works

                What this is

                An inject is a scenario card: one realistic problem dropped into the exercise, like a flooded polling place or a fake text telling voters the election moved.

                A tabletop exercise is designed to find the gaps in your planning. That is the whole product. Finding one is the exercise working, not you failing.

                This is a safe space. Nothing written here is an evaluation of anyone, and nothing leaves the room except the action items you decide to take with you.

                The goal is to walk out with renewed confidence and a short list of things to go fix.

                Two games, scored separately

                There are two things going on here and they do not share a scoreboard.

                Ballotship, the board

                Each jurisdiction hides seven sites on a six-by-six grid. Every round you aim a footprint at the other team's grid without knowing where anything is, and they aim one at yours. Cells get hit, sites go offline, and whoever has knocked out more of the other jurisdiction when the rounds run out wins that part.

                This half is luck and deduction. It is a hunt across a hidden map. It is not a measure of how well your office is run, and a site going offline is not a prediction that it would.

                The inject response, the real one

                The other half is what you write when an inject lands on you. Who owns it, who gets called, what you do in the first hour, what policy it rests on, what goes out and to whom, and what you needed that you did not have. That is the part that gets read back in the retro, and it is the part that turns into action items.

                It is not scored during play at all. Nothing you write can save a cell, and nothing you write can lose one.

                You can win one and lose the other, and that happens often. A team can get lucky on the board and write thin responses all afternoon. A team can get picked apart on the grid and still walk out with the better plan, which is the outcome that actually matters on Election Day. When the final screen names a winner, it is naming the winner of the board. The retro settles the rest.

                Objectives

                1. Analyze current election preparedness processes to identify opportunities for stronger interagency coordination, formalized procedures and resource integration.
                2. Evaluate and identify the roles and capabilities of participating departments to improve information sharing, collaboration and mutual understanding across agencies.
                3. Create department-specific action items and planning enhancements that can be folded into existing emergency response plans, election support plans and the 2026 Midterm Election Action Plan.
                4. Assess organizational readiness and collaborative decision-making to strengthen confidence in supporting election administration before and during Election Day.

                Ground rules

                Do not fight the exercise

                You will find things the scenario does not tell you. Make a reasonable assumption, say out loud what you assumed, and keep going. Arguing with the premise burns the clock and teaches you nothing. If an assumption changes your answer, that is worth writing in the gap box.

                Play yourself

                Answer in your real role, with the authority you actually have and the resources you actually have. If the person who would normally make this call is not in the room, say who that is and what you would do to reach them. That gap is a finding.

                Everyone in your office is available and well

                Build your scenario around systems, facilities, timing and information. Do not solve a problem by having a colleague, a supervisor or anyone else in the room be harmed, removed or otherwise unavailable. Staffing continuity comes up in its own inject, and it is handled there.

                No synthetic voices or faces

                Do not generate a synthetic voice or face of a named real person.

                Work it together

                The response boxes ask who owns it and who else has to know because that is the part most plans are thin on. Talk it through as a group before anyone types.

                Nothing you make here leaves the room

                The AI piece produces material that is supposed to look convincing. Label it as exercise material, keep it in the room, and delete it after the retro.

                A round, step by step

                1. Both teams get the same inject at the same time.
                2. On the first clock, each team writes how it would run that situation against the other jurisdiction, and builds one piece of it with an AI tool.
                3. The two attacks swap automatically.
                4. On the second clock, each team answers the attack it received, using the response boxes.
                5. The round resolves on both boards at once and the next inject comes up.

                Ten minutes a phase, so about 23 minutes a round with the result screen. Round 1 always runs long while everyone learns the boxes, so expect to add time or treat it as practice.
